THE NUMBERS AND WHERE THEY COME FROM (for replies)
• 134°F (57°C), Furnace Creek, 10 July 1913 (NPS). Still the WMO's official world record, though some climatologists doubt the 1913 reading — hence "officially".
• Ground: 201°F at Furnace Creek, 15 July 1972 (NPS). Water boils at 212°F at sea level: 11°F short.
• Above 100°F, drink 1 litre every hour (US Army FM 3-05.70, desert survival chapter). The park asks for at least a gallon (4 L) a day.
• Summer nights: lows "often still above 100°F" (NPS summer guide).
• About 2 days: the military desert-survival table (Adolph, Physiology of Man in the Desert, 1947, reproduced by the FAA): at a 120°F shade maximum, resting in shade, 2 days with no water and 2 days with 1 quart. At 110°F it is 3 days. An average for healthy adults; people vary.
• The name: the lost '49ers of the Bennett-Arcan party crossed at Christmas 1849; leaving, one is said to have called out "Goodbye, Death Valley". Only one man had died during their long wait (NPS).
