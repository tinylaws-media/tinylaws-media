# Could you survive? #02 — Everest summit: fact sheet (checked 29 Oct 2026)

Each number used in the episode, with its source. "Spoken" is what the narration says; "screen" what the HUD shows.
Main assumption of the episode: she **spawns** on the summit — no weeks of acclimatisation, no bottled oxygen, like a
pilot whose cabin suddenly loses pressure. Real climbers who adapt for weeks can stand there (Messner and Habeler did
it without bottled oxygen in 1978); the episode says so.

| Claim | Spoken / screen | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Height 8,848.86 m (29,032 ft), announced jointly by Nepal and China on 8 Dec 2020; rounded 8,849 m | "eight thousand, eight hundred forty-nine meters" · screen 8,849 m | [Al Jazeera, 8 Dec 2020](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2020/12/8/mt-everest-nepal-china-announce-revised-height-at-8848-metres) | High |
| Barometric pressure on the summit measured at 253 Torr (sea level 760 Torr) → 33 % of sea level; air is 20.9 % oxygen at every height, so each breath carries about a third of the oxygen | "each breath has about a third of the oxygen you're used to" · screen O₂ 33 % | West et al., *J Appl Physiol* 1983, via PubMed ([DOI 10.1152/jappl.1983.55.3.678](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1983.55.3.678)); [Wikipedia, Death zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_zone) ("less than a third of its sea-level value") | High |
| "Death zone" = above about 8,000 m, term from Swiss doctor Edouard Wyss-Dunant (1953) | screen "DEATH ZONE 8,000 m" on the altimeter | [Wikipedia, Death zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_zone) | High |
| Climbers spend about 3–4 weeks of acclimatisation rotations from base camp before a summit push (20–28 days) | "Real climbers spend weeks adapting first" | [Alan Arnette, "Why does it take so long to climb Everest?"](https://www.alanarnette.com/blog/?p=25602) (citing the Himalayan Database for ~40 days after base camp) | Medium-high (expedition practice, not a law of nature) |
| Summit temperature: January average −36 °C (−33 °F), can drop to −60 °C; July average about −19 °C | "the summit averages minus thirty-six in January" · screen −36 °C / −33 °F | [Britannica (Student), Mount Everest](https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/Mount-Everest/274233) | High (averages; season stated) |
| Sustained winds of more than 100 mph (160 km/h) at the peak (jet stream) | "winds up there can top a hundred miles an hour" · screen 160+ km/h | Britannica (Student), same page; National Geographic says the 2022 station was built for "200-plus mile an hour winds" ([NatGeo](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/adventure/article/mount-everest-highest-weather-station-perpetual-planet)) | High for "can top 100 mph" |
| Near the summit, wind chill is always below −30 °C; in the spring season typical wind chill −50 °C and facial frostbite time about 5 min (about 1 min in severe storms) | "in that wind, bare skin can freeze in under five minutes" · screen < 5 MIN (fixed after review: 5 min is the typical spring value, ~1 min in storms, so "under five" is the honest wording for 100+ mph wind) | Moore & Semple, *High Alt Med Biol* 2011, via PubMed ([DOI 10.1089/ham.2011.0008](https://doi.org/10.1089/ham.2011.0008)) | High (modelled typical value for spring; say "about") |
| Time of useful consciousness without oxygen (FAA): 28,000 ft 2.5–3 min; 30,000 ft 1–2 min. The summit (29,032 ft) sits between → **about 1–3 minutes** of clear thinking for someone exposed suddenly, without acclimatisation | "So how long would you last? … About one to three minutes of clear thinking." · screen ~1–3 MIN, "IF YOU SPAWN WITHOUT ADAPTING" | FAA *Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge*, table reproduced by [AOPA](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2014/november/pilot/pe_safety); same values in [Wikipedia, Time of useful consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_of_useful_consciousness) (FAA AC 61-107) | Medium-high: an estimate for sudden exposure, varies by person; it is the time before judgement fails, not a time of death; said with "about" |
| Climbers measured near the summit could still do simple tasks (acclimatised) | supports "real climbers who adapt can stand here" | West et al. 1983 (above) | High |
| First ascent without bottled oxygen: Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler, 8 May 1978 | "In 1978, two climbers stood here without bottled oxygen, after weeks of adapting" (names in the caption kit only, never drawn) | [Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/first-ascent-of-mt-everest-without-oxygen) | High |
| The summit rock is marine limestone (Qomolangma Formation), Ordovician, about 450–470 million years old, with fossils of sea animals (trilobites, brachiopods, ostracods, crinoids) | "This rock was sea floor. It still holds sea fossils, over four hundred million years old." · screen 400+ MILLION YEARS (fossils are mostly fragments: the drawing is a cartoon, the voice does not say "packed") | [IUGS Geoheritage, "The Ordovician rocks of Mount Everest"](https://iugs-geoheritage.org/?p=3648) (~450 Myr, Middle Ordovician, "the highest rocks in the planet are fossiliferous, marine limestone"); [Montana State University, Everest education site](https://montana.edu/everest/facts/summit-limestone.html) (seafloor 470 Myr ago; trilobites, brachiopods, ostracods, crinoids) | High ("over four hundred million" covers both) |

Not used, not verified well enough or left out on purpose: the number of deaths and bodies on the mountain (no
graphic content, and the count changes yearly); "planes fly at that height" (cruise altitudes vary, 29,032 ft is
below most airliner cruise levels); the boiling point (about 71 °C from 253 Torr, our arithmetic: correct but cut for
time); a specific wind record.
